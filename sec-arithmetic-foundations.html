    Section 1.2 Arithmetic Foundations

    Prerequisite. Familiarity with the mathematical language and notation introduced in Section 1.1.
    🔗

    Objectives: Learning Objectives

    By the end of this section, the reader should be able to:
    🔗
    • interpret addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use the commutative, associative, distributive, identity, and inverse properties;
      🔗
      🔗
    • perform arithmetic with positive and negative numbers;
      🔗
      🔗
    • work with fractions and mixed arithmetic expressions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use ratios, rates, proportions, and percentages;
      🔗
      🔗
    • apply exponent laws and interpret negative and rational exponents;
      🔗
      🔗
    • simplify and interpret radicals;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use scientific notation;
      🔗
      🔗
    • apply the standard order of operations;
      🔗
      🔗
    • estimate quantities and check whether calculations are reasonable;
      🔗
      🔗
    • recognize common arithmetic errors that later affect algebra and calculus.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.1 Arithmetic and Mathematical Structure

    Arithmetic studies numbers and the operations performed on them. At an elementary level, arithmetic provides methods for calculation. At a deeper level, however, arithmetic introduces structural properties that reappear throughout algebra, analysis, number theory, and other branches of mathematics.
    🔗
    The four fundamental arithmetic operations are:
    🔗
    • addition;
      🔗
      🔗
    • subtraction;
      🔗
      🔗
    • multiplication;
      🔗
      🔗
    • division.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    These operations do not behave independently. They are connected through inverse operations and governed by properties such as commutativity, associativity, and distributivity.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.2 Addition

    Definition 1.2.1. Addition.

    Addition combines quantities to produce a quantity called their sum.
    🔗
    🔗
    The expression
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+b \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read “\(a\) plus \(b\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    The symbol \(+\) is the plus sign.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 7+5=12. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Here \(7\) and \(5\) are called addends, and \(12\) is their sum.
    🔗
    Addition may represent combining collections, increasing a quantity, accumulating change, or combining mathematical objects with an appropriate addition operation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.3 Subtraction

    Definition 1.2.2. Subtraction.

    Subtraction finds the difference between quantities and may be interpreted as addition of an additive inverse.
    🔗
    🔗
    The expression
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a-b \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read “\(a\) minus \(b\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    The symbol \(-\) is the minus sign.
    🔗
    The first quantity is the minuend, the second is the subtrahend, and the result is the difference.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 11-4=7. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Subtraction can also be written as addition:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a-b=a+(-b). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This interpretation becomes particularly important in algebra.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.4 The Minus Sign Has Multiple Roles

    The minus sign can represent several related ideas.
    🔗
    In
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 8-3, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    it indicates subtraction.
    🔗
    In
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -5, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    it indicates the opposite, or additive inverse, of \(5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -x, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    it represents the additive inverse of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, the same written symbol must sometimes be interpreted according to its position in an expression.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.5 Multiplication

    Definition 1.2.3. Multiplication.

    Multiplication combines quantities through repeated addition, scaling, or a more general product operation.
    🔗
    🔗
    Multiplication may be written in several forms:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\times b,\qquad a\cdot b,\qquad ab. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The symbol \(\times\) is the multiplication sign, and \(\cdot\) is the multiplication dot.
    🔗
    The quantities being multiplied are called factors. Their result is called the product.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4\cdot7=28. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In algebra, multiplication is often written by juxtaposition:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4x=4\cdot x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.6 Multiplication as Scaling

    Multiplication is more general than repeated addition.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac12(8)=4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    scales \(8\) by a factor of one-half.
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1.5(10)=15 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    scales \(10\) by a factor of \(1.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This interpretation becomes especially useful when studying functions, geometry, vectors, probability, and linear algebra.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.7 Division

    Definition 1.2.4. Division.

    Division determines how one quantity is distributed relative to another nonzero quantity and may be interpreted as multiplication by a reciprocal.
    🔗
    🔗
    Division may be written as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\div b,\qquad \frac ab,\qquad a/b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The quantity being divided is the dividend, the quantity dividing it is the divisor, and the result is the quotient.
    🔗
    For \(b\neq0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab=a\left(\frac1b\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus division by \(b\) is equivalent to multiplication by the reciprocal of \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.8 Division by Zero

    Division by zero is undefined in ordinary arithmetic.
    🔗
    Suppose one attempted to define
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac10=x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Multiplying both sides by \(0\) would suggest
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1=0x=0, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    which is impossible.
    🔗
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac10 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    has no ordinary real or complex value.
    🔗

    Warning 1.2.5. Division by Zero.

    A denominator may never equal zero in ordinary arithmetic.
    🔗
    The expression
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a}{0} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is undefined.
    🔗
    The expression \(0/0\) is also undefined. In calculus, \(0/0\) may appear as an indeterminate form in a limit, but this does not mean that division by zero becomes valid.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.9 Inverse Operations

    Addition and subtraction are inverse operations.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 7+4=11 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    can be reversed by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 11-4=7. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Multiplication and division are also inverse operations when the divisor is nonzero:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5\cdot6=30,\qquad 30\div6=5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This idea of reversing an operation later develops into inverse functions and inverse algebraic operations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.10 The Commutative Property

    Definition 1.2.6. Commutative Property.

    An operation is commutative when changing the order of the operands does not change the result.
    🔗
    🔗
    Addition is commutative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+b=b+a. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Multiplication is commutative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ab=ba. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4+9=9+4,\qquad 3\cdot8=8\cdot3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Subtraction is generally not commutative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 8-3\neq3-8. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Division is generally not commutative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac84\neq\frac48. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.11 The Associative Property

    Definition 1.2.7. Associative Property.

    An operation is associative when changing the grouping of repeated operations does not change the result.
    🔗
    🔗
    Addition is associative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)+c=a+(b+c). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Multiplication is associative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (ab)c=a(bc). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (2+3)+4=2+(3+4)=9. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Subtraction and division are generally not associative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (10-5)-2\neq10-(5-2). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.12 The Distributive Property

    Definition 1.2.8. Distributive Property.

    The distributive property connects multiplication with addition or subtraction.
    🔗
    🔗
    For addition,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(b+c)=ab+ac. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For subtraction,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(b-c)=ab-ac. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3(5+2)=3(5)+3(2)=15+6=21. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The distributive property is one of the most frequently used algebraic properties in mathematics.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.13 Identity Elements

    Definition 1.2.9. Additive Identity.

    The additive identity is a number that leaves another number unchanged under addition.
    🔗
    🔗
    For ordinary arithmetic, the additive identity is \(0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+0=0+a=a. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 1.2.10. Multiplicative Identity.

    The multiplicative identity is a number that leaves another number unchanged under multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗
    For ordinary arithmetic, the multiplicative identity is \(1\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\cdot1=1\cdot a=a. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    These concepts later generalize to identity elements in abstract algebra.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.14 Additive Inverses

    Definition 1.2.11. Additive Inverse.

    The additive inverse of \(a\) is the number \(-a\) satisfying
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+(-a)=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Examples include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5+(-5)=0,\qquad -12+12=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A number and its additive inverse are often called opposites.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.15 Multiplicative Inverses

    Definition 1.2.12. Multiplicative Inverse.

    For \(a\neq0\text{,}\) the multiplicative inverse, or reciprocal, of \(a\) is \(1/a\text{,}\) because
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\left(\frac1a\right)=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, the reciprocal of \(5\) is \(1/5\text{,}\) while the reciprocal of \(2/3\) is \(3/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Zero has no multiplicative inverse.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.16 Zero Properties

    Several basic properties of zero are used constantly:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+0=a,\qquad a-0=a,\qquad a\cdot0=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For \(a\neq0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac0a=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    However,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac a0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is undefined.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.17 Positive and Negative Numbers

    A positive number is greater than zero, while a negative number is less than zero.
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a>0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    means \(a\) is positive, and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a<0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    means \(a\) is negative.
    🔗
    Zero is neither positive nor negative.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.18 Addition with Signed Numbers

    When two numbers have the same sign, add their magnitudes and keep the common sign:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5+3=8,\qquad (-5)+(-3)=-8. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    When the signs differ, subtract the smaller magnitude from the larger and use the sign of the number having greater magnitude:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 8+(-3)=5,\qquad 3+(-8)=-5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.19 Subtraction with Signed Numbers

    Subtraction can always be rewritten as addition of the opposite:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a-b=a+(-b). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 7-(-4)=7+4=11. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -3-5=-3+(-5)=-8. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.20 Multiplication and Division with Signed Numbers

    The sign rules for multiplication are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (+)(+)=+,\qquad (+)(-)=-,\qquad (-)(+)=-,\qquad (-)(-)=+. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The same sign rules apply to division.
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (-4)(-6)=24,\qquad (-20)\div5=-4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.21 Absolute Value

    Definition 1.2.13. Absolute Value.

    The absolute value of a real number \(x\text{,}\) written \(\left|x\right|\text{,}\) is its distance from zero on the real number line.
    🔗
    🔗
    The vertical bars in
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left|x\right| \end{equation*}
    🔗
    are called absolute-value bars in this context.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left|5\right|=5,\qquad \left|-5\right|=5,\qquad \left|0\right|=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Algebraically,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left|x\right|=\begin{cases}x,&x\geq0,\\-x,&x<0.\end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Absolute value is developed further as a distance concept in the section on number systems.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.22 Fractions

    Definition 1.2.14. Fraction.

    A fraction is an expression of the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab,\qquad b\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The quantity \(a\) is the numerator and \(b\) is the denominator.
    🔗
    🔗
    The fraction bar represents division:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab=a\div b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Fractions can represent parts of a whole, ratios, quotients, probabilities, rates, or exact numerical values.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.23 Equivalent Fractions

    Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number does not change a fraction’s value:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab=\frac{ka}{kb},\qquad k\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac12=\frac24=\frac36=\frac{50}{100}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.24 Reducing Fractions

    A fraction is in lowest terms when its numerator and denominator have no common factor greater than \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{18}{24}=\frac34. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The numerator and denominator were both divided by \(6\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Cancellation.

    Cancellation is division by a common nonzero factor.
    🔗
    It does not apply across addition.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{6x}{6y}=\frac xy \end{equation*}
    🔗
    when the denominators involved are nonzero, but
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x+6}{x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    does not allow the \(x\)’s to be canceled because the numerator is a sum, not a product having \(x\) as a common factor.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.25 Adding Fractions

    Fractions require a common denominator before their numerators can be added.
    🔗
    If the denominators are equal,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab+\frac cb=\frac{a+c}{b}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For different nonzero denominators,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab+\frac cd=\frac{ad+bc}{bd}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Usually a least common denominator is preferred when convenient.
    🔗

    Example 1.2.15. Adding Fractions.

    Compute
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac34+\frac56. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{19}{12} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.26 Subtracting Fractions

    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab-\frac cd=\frac{ad-bc}{bd}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac56-\frac14=\frac{10}{12}-\frac3{12}=\frac7{12}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.27 Multiplying Fractions

    Fractions are multiplied by multiplying numerators and denominators:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab\cdot\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac23\cdot\frac9{10}=\frac{18}{30}=\frac35. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Common factors may often be canceled before multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.28 Dividing Fractions

    Dividing by a nonzero fraction is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab\div\frac cd=\frac ab\cdot\frac dc=\frac{ad}{bc}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This requires
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} b\neq0,\qquad c\neq0,\qquad d\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.29 Complex Fractions

    A complex fraction is a fraction containing fractions in its numerator, denominator, or both.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\frac23}{\frac57}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This can be simplified using division of fractions:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\frac23}{\frac57}=\frac23\cdot\frac75=\frac{14}{15}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.30 Ratios

    Definition 1.2.17. Ratio.

    A ratio compares two quantities through division.
    🔗
    🔗
    The ratio of \(a\) to \(b\) may be written
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a:b \end{equation*}
    🔗
    or
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The colon \(: \) is called a colon. In ratio notation, \(a:b\) is read “\(a\) to \(b\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    For example, a class containing \(12\) students in one group and \(8\) in another has ratio
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 12:8=3:2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.31 Rates

    Definition 1.2.18. Rate.

    A rate is a ratio comparing quantities measured in different units.
    🔗
    🔗
    Examples include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 60\text{ miles}/2\text{ hours},\qquad \$120/8\text{ hours}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.32 Unit Rates

    Definition 1.2.19. Unit Rate.

    A unit rate is a rate whose second quantity has numerical value \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{180\text{ miles}}{3\text{ hours}}=60\text{ miles per hour}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.33 Proportions

    Definition 1.2.20. Proportion.

    A proportion is an equation stating that two ratios are equal.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac23=\frac{x}{12}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For nonzero denominators,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab=\frac cd \end{equation*}
    🔗
    implies
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ad=bc. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This follows by multiplying both sides by \(bd\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.2.21. Solving a Proportion.

    Solve
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac35=\frac{x}{20}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} x=12 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.34 Percentages

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \% \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is called the percent sign.
    🔗
    The word percent means “per hundred.”
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 35\%=\frac{35}{100}=0.35. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 7\%=0.07,\qquad 125\%=1.25. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A percentage may be greater than \(100\%\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.35 Finding a Percentage of a Quantity

    To find \(p\%\) of a quantity \(A\text{,}\) convert the percentage to a decimal or fraction and multiply:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p\%\text{ of }A=\frac{p}{100}A. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 15\%\text{ of }80=0.15(80)=12. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.36 Percent Increase and Decrease

    If a quantity changes from an original value \(O\neq0\) to a new value \(N\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{percent change}=\frac{N-O}{O}\times100\%. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A positive result represents an increase and a negative result represents a decrease.
    🔗

    Example 1.2.23. Percent Increase.

    A quantity increases from \(80\) to \(92\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    The quantity increased by \(15\%\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.37 Percentage Points

    A change between percentages can be described in percentage points.
    🔗
    For example, an increase from \(20\%\) to \(25\%\) is an increase of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 25\%-20\%=5 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    percentage points.
    🔗
    This is different from the relative percent increase:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{25-20}{20}\times100\%=25\%. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.38 Exponents

    Definition 1.2.25. Exponent.

    An exponent indicates repeated multiplication of a base.
    🔗
    🔗
    For positive integer \(n\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^n=\underbrace{a\cdot a\cdots a}_{n\text{ factors}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In \(a^n\text{,}\) \(a\) is the base and \(n\) is the exponent.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2^4=2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2=16. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.39 Product Rule for Exponents

    When multiplying powers having the same base,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^ma^n=a^{m+n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^3x^5=x^8. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The exponents are added because the total number of factors of \(x\) is \(3+5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.40 Quotient Rule for Exponents

    For \(a\neq0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^7}{x^3}=x^4,\qquad x\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.41 Power of a Power

    \begin{equation*} (a^m)^n=a^{mn}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (x^3)^4=x^{12}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.42 Power of a Product

    \begin{equation*} (ab)^n=a^nb^n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (2x)^3=2^3x^3=8x^3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.43 Power of a Quotient

    For \(b\neq0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac ab\right)^n=\frac{a^n}{b^n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.44 The Zero Exponent

    For \(a\neq0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^0=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This is consistent with the quotient rule:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a^n}{a^n}=a^{n-n}=a^0=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The restriction \(a\neq0\) is important.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.45 Negative Exponents

    For \(a\neq0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2^{-3}=\frac1{2^3}=\frac18. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A negative exponent does not make a quantity negative. Instead, it indicates a reciprocal.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.46 Common Exponent Errors

    Exponent rules apply to multiplication, not generally to addition.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)^2\neq a^2+b^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Instead,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^m+a^n \end{equation*}
    🔗
    cannot generally be simplified by adding the exponents.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.47 Radicals

    Definition 1.2.26. Radical.

    A radical expression uses a root operation, commonly written with the radical symbol \(\sqrt{\phantom{x}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The square root of \(a\) is written
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt a. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    More generally,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[n]{a} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    denotes an \(n\)-th root.
    🔗
    The quantity \(a\) inside the radical is called the radicand.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.48 Principal Square Roots

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt a \end{equation*}
    🔗
    denotes the nonnegative square root when \(a\geq0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt9=3, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    not \(\pm3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    However, the equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=9 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    has two real solutions:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\pm3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The symbol \(\pm\) is the plus-or-minus sign and is read “plus or minus.”
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.49 Square Roots of Squares

    For real \(a\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{a^2}=\left|a\right|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The absolute value is necessary because the principal square root is nonnegative.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{(-5)^2}=\sqrt{25}=5=\left|-5\right|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.50 Product Property of Radicals

    For nonnegative real numbers \(a,b\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{ab}=\sqrt a\sqrt b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{36\cdot2}=6\sqrt2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.51 Simplifying Radicals

    Perfect-power factors can be removed from radicals.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36\cdot2}=6\sqrt2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.52 Radicals Do Not Distribute over Addition

    In general,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{a+b}\neq\sqrt a+\sqrt b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{9+16}=5, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    while
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt9+\sqrt{16}=3+4=7. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.53 Rational Exponents

    Roots can be represented using fractional exponents.
    🔗
    For suitable values,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    More generally,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 27^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{27}\right)^2=3^2=9. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Domain restrictions depend on whether the root index is even or odd and on the number system being used.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.54 Scientific Notation

    Definition 1.2.27. Scientific Notation.

    A nonzero number is written in scientific notation in the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\times10^n,\qquad 1\leq\left|a\right|<10, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The multiplication sign \(\times\) is commonly used here because juxtaposition could make the decimal coefficient and power of ten difficult to distinguish.
    🔗
    Examples include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4500000=4.5\times10^6 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0.00032=3.2\times10^{-4}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.55 Operations in Scientific Notation

    To multiply quantities in scientific notation, multiply their coefficients and add the powers of ten:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a\times10^m)(b\times10^n)=ab\times10^{m+n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (3\times10^4)(2\times10^5)=6\times10^9. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For division,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a\times10^m}{b\times10^n}=\frac ab\times10^{m-n},\qquad b\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.56 Order of Operations

    When an expression contains several operations, a standard convention determines the order in which they are performed.
    🔗
    A useful order is:
    🔗
    1. evaluate grouping symbols;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. evaluate exponents and roots;
      🔗
      🔗
    3. perform multiplication and division from left to right;
      🔗
      🔗
    4. perform addition and subtraction from left to right.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Multiplication and division have equal priority.
    🔗
    Addition and subtraction also have equal priority.
    🔗
    Therefore, operations of equal priority are evaluated from left to right.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.57 A Common Order-of-Operations Error

    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 24\div6\cdot2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Division and multiplication have equal priority, so evaluate from left to right:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 24\div6\cdot2=4\cdot2=8. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    It is incorrect to automatically perform every multiplication before every division.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.58 Unary Negatives and Exponents

    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -3^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The exponent applies to \(3\) before the leading negative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -3^2=-(3^2)=-9. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    By contrast,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (-3)^2=9. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Parentheses change the base being squared.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.59 Worked Example: Order of Operations

    Example 1.2.28. Evaluate an Arithmetic Expression.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3+4(2^2)-\frac{6}{3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} 17 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.60 Exact Values and Decimal Approximations

    Whenever practical, mathematics often preserves an exact value until an approximation is actually needed.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac13 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is exact, whereas
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0.333 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is only an approximation.
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is exact, while
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2\approx1.41421. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Early rounding can introduce accumulated error into later calculations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.61 Rounding

    Rounding replaces a number by a nearby value having fewer digits.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3.14159265\approx3.142 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    to three decimal places.
    🔗
    The symbol \(\approx\) is appropriate because the rounded value is not exactly equal to the original quantity.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.62 Estimation

    Definition 1.2.30. Estimation.

    Estimation replaces quantities by nearby, easier values to obtain an approximate result or check the reasonableness of an exact calculation.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 19.8(5.1)\approx20(5)=100. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, an exact calculator result near \(100\) is reasonable.
    🔗
    A result such as \(1000\) would signal a likely arithmetic or input error.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.63 Units in Arithmetic

    Quantities with units require additional care.
    🔗
    Compatible quantities can be added:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3\text{ m}+5\text{ m}=8\text{ m}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    But quantities with incompatible dimensions cannot ordinarily be added:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3\text{ m}+5\text{ s} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    has no standard physical meaning.
    🔗
    Multiplication and division combine units:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{120\text{ miles}}{2\text{ hours}}=60\text{ miles/hour}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This principle becomes important in applied mathematics and mathematical modeling.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.64 Arithmetic Properties Summary

    For suitable numbers,
    🔗
    These properties are not merely computational rules. They provide an early example of the structural laws later studied abstractly in algebra.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.65 Common Arithmetic Errors

    Adding Denominators.

    In general,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ab+\frac cd\neq\frac{a+c}{b+d}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A common denominator is required.
    🔗
    🔗

    Canceling Terms Instead of Factors.

    Cancellation applies to common factors, not pieces separated by addition or subtraction.
    🔗
    🔗

    Distributing Exponents over Addition.

    In general,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)^n\neq a^n+b^n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Ignoring Parentheses.

    The expressions
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -x^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad (-x)^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    are different.
    🔗
    🔗

    Dividing by Zero.

    Any algebraic manipulation that divides by a quantity requires checking that the quantity is nonzero.
    🔗
    🔗

    Rounding Too Early.

    Repeated approximation may produce a noticeably inaccurate final result. Keep exact values when possible and round near the end of a calculation.
