    Section 1.8 Methods of Proof

    Remark 1.8.1. Prerequisites.

    Familiarity with propositions, implications, quantifiers, and logical equivalence from Section 1.7, together with sets, relations, and functions from the preceding sections.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.8.2. Learning Objectives.

    By the end of this section, the reader should be able to:
    🔗
    • distinguish mathematical evidence from mathematical proof;
      🔗
      🔗
    • identify hypotheses and conclusions in theorem statements;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use definitions as tools in proofs;
      🔗
      🔗
    • construct direct proofs;
      🔗
      🔗
    • prove statements by contraposition and contradiction;
      🔗
      🔗
    • organize proofs by cases;
      🔗
      🔗
    • prove existence and uniqueness statements;
      🔗
      🔗
    • distinguish constructive and nonconstructive existence proofs;
      🔗
      🔗
    • disprove universal statements using counterexamples;
      🔗
      🔗
    • prove biconditional statements;
      🔗
      🔗
    • prove set containments and equalities;
      🔗
      🔗
    • prove basic statements involving functions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use mathematical induction and strong induction;
      🔗
      🔗
    • recognize recursive definitions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • select an appropriate proof strategy for a given statement;
      🔗
      🔗
    • identify common logical and structural errors in proofs.
      🔗
      🔗

    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.1 What Is a Mathematical Proof?

    A mathematical proof is a logically valid argument establishing that a mathematical statement must be true under the stated assumptions.
    🔗
    A proof does more than provide evidence.
    🔗
    It explains why the conclusion follows necessarily from definitions, assumptions, previously established results, and valid logical reasoning.
    🔗

    Definition 1.8.3. Mathematical Proof.

    A mathematical proof is a finite sequence of logically justified statements that establishes the truth of a mathematical claim.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.2 Evidence versus Proof

    Examples can provide evidence for a mathematical statement, but examples alone cannot prove a universal claim.
    🔗
    Suppose one suspects that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n^2+n \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is even for every integer \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Checking
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1^2+1=2,\qquad 2^2+2=6,\qquad 3^2+3=12 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    provides evidence.
    🔗
    However, infinitely many integers remain unchecked.
    🔗
    A proof must establish the result for an arbitrary integer.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.3 The Role of Definitions

    Definitions are among the most important tools in proof writing.
    🔗
    For example, the statement
    🔗
    “@@PROTECTED0@@ is even”
    🔗
    has the precise meaning
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n=2k \end{equation*}
    🔗
    for some integer \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    “@@PROTECTED0@@ is odd”
    🔗
    means
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n=2k+1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    for some integer \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A proof often begins by replacing terminology with its formal definition.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.4 Theorem Structure

    Many theorems have the logical form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P\to Q. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Here \(P\) is the hypothesis and \(Q\) is the conclusion.
    🔗
    For example:
    🔗
    \begin{theorem} If \(n\) is an even integer, then \(n^2\) is even. \end{theorem}
    🔗
    The hypothesis is
    🔗
    \(n\) is even.
    🔗
    The conclusion is
    🔗
    \(n^2\) is even.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.5 Beginning a Proof

    When proving a universal implication such as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \forall x\in A,\;P(x)\to Q(x), \end{equation*}
    🔗
    a standard beginning is:
    🔗
    1. let \(x\in A\) be arbitrary;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. assume \(P(x)\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. use the hypothesis and known facts;
      🔗
      🔗
    4. establish \(Q(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The word arbitrary matters.
    🔗
    It indicates that the argument does not depend on a special choice of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.6 Direct Proof

    Definition 1.8.4. Direct Proof.

    A direct proof of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P\to Q \end{equation*}
    🔗
    begins by assuming \(P\) and proceeds logically until \(Q\) is established.
    🔗
    🔗
    This is often the most natural proof method when the hypothesis can be translated directly into useful algebraic or structural information.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.7 Worked Example: Direct Proof

    Example 1.8.5. The Sum of Two Even Integers Is Even.

    Prove that if \(a\) and \(b\) are even integers, then \(a+b\) is even.

    Subsection 1.8.8 Writing the Arbitrary Element

    A proof involving “every” or “for all” usually requires an arbitrary element.
    🔗
    For example, to prove
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\subseteq B, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    one begins:
    🔗
    Let \(x\in A\) be arbitrary.
    🔗
    The goal is then to show
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\in B. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Because \(x\) was arbitrary, the result holds for every element of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.9 Proof by Cases

    Sometimes the hypothesis naturally divides into several possibilities.
    🔗

    Definition 1.8.6. Proof by Cases.

    A proof by cases divides the problem into exhaustive cases and proves the desired conclusion separately in each case.
    🔗
    🔗
    The cases must collectively cover every possibility permitted by the hypothesis.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.10 Worked Example: Proof by Cases

    Example 1.8.7. The Square of an Integer Has Predictable Parity.

    Prove that for every integer \(n\text{,}\) \(n^2+n\) is even.
    🔗
    Every integer is either even or odd.
    🔗
    Case 1: @@PROTECTED0@@ is even.
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n=2k \end{equation*}
    🔗
    for some \(k\in\Z\text{.}\) Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n^2+n=(2k)^2+2k=4k^2+2k=2(2k^2+k), \end{equation*}
    🔗
    which is even.
    🔗
    Case 2: @@PROTECTED0@@ is odd.
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n=2k+1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n^2+n=(2k+1)^2+(2k+1)=4k^2+6k+2=2(2k^2+3k+1), \end{equation*}
    🔗
    which is also even.
    🔗
    Solution.
    🔗
    Therefore \(n^2+n\) is even for every integer \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.11 Exhaustive Cases

    A case proof is valid only when all possibilities are covered.
    🔗
    For integers, the cases
    🔗
    even or odd
    🔗
    are exhaustive.
    🔗
    Similarly, a real number satisfies exactly one of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x<0,\qquad x=0,\qquad x>0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Leaving out one possibility creates an incomplete proof.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.12 Proof by Contraposition

    Recall the logical equivalence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P\to Q\equiv\neg Q\to\neg P. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 1.8.8. Proof by Contraposition.

    To prove
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P\to Q, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    one may instead prove the logically equivalent contrapositive
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \neg Q\to\neg P. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This strategy is useful when the negation of the conclusion gives more usable information than the original hypothesis.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.13 Worked Example: Contraposition

    Example 1.8.9. If \(n^2\) Is Odd, Then \(n\) Is Odd.

    Instead of proving the statement directly, prove its contrapositive:
    🔗
    If \(n\) is even, then \(n^2\) is even.

    🔗
    The contrapositive is true, so the original implication is true.
    🔗
    Solution.
    🔗
    If \(n^2\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.14 Choosing Contraposition

    Contraposition is especially useful when the conclusion says that something has a property such as:
    🔗
    • being irrational;
      🔗
      🔗
    • being nonzero;
      🔗
      🔗
    • being injective;
      🔗
      🔗
    • not belonging to a set;
      🔗
      🔗
    • failing to satisfy some condition.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Negating such conclusions often produces a concrete assumption that is easier to manipulate.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.15 Proof by Contradiction

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \bot \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is called the contradiction symbol or bottom symbol and is often read
    🔗
    “contradiction.”
    🔗

    Definition 1.8.10. Proof by Contradiction.

    To prove a statement \(P\) by contradiction, assume \(\neg P\) and logically derive an impossibility or contradiction.
    🔗
    🔗
    Symbolically,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \neg P\Longrightarrow\bot. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore the assumption \(\neg P\) must be false, and \(P\) must be true.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.16 What Counts as a Contradiction?

    A contradiction may appear as:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0=1, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a<a, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n\text{ is both even and odd}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    or a statement that directly conflicts with an earlier assumption.
    🔗
    The contradiction must arise logically from the negation of the claim and accepted mathematical facts.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.17 Worked Example: Irrationality of \(\sqrt2\)

    Example 1.8.11. Prove \(\sqrt2\) Is Irrational.

    Assume for contradiction that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2=\frac ab \end{equation*}
    🔗
    for integers \(a,b\) with \(b\neq0\text{,}\) where \(a/b\) is in lowest terms.

    🔗
    Thus both \(a\) and \(b\) are divisible by \(2\text{,}\) contradicting the assumption that \(a/b\) was in lowest terms.
    🔗
    Solution.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2\notin\Q \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.18 Contraposition versus Contradiction

    These methods are related but distinct.
    🔗
    To prove
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P\to Q \end{equation*}
    🔗
    by contraposition, prove
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \neg Q\to\neg P. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To prove the same statement by contradiction, assume
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P\land\neg Q \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and derive a contradiction.
    🔗
    Contraposition often produces a more direct argument.
    🔗
    Contradiction is useful when the negation of the desired statement has strong structural consequences.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.19 Existence Proofs

    An existence statement has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \exists x\in A,\;P(x). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To prove it, one must establish that at least one suitable object exists.
    🔗
    There are two broad approaches:

    Subsection 1.8.25 Disproving Statements

    Not every mathematical claim is true.
    🔗
    A universal statement
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \forall x\in A,\;P(x) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is disproved by finding one element \(a\in A\) for which \(P(a)\) is false.

    Such an object is called a counterexample.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.8.16. Counterexample.

    A counterexample is a specific example showing that a universal statement is false.
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗
    Every prime number is odd.
    🔗
    The number
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is prime and even.
    🔗

    🔗

    To prove a universal claim, one must handle every possible case.
    🔗
    To disprove a universal claim, one counterexample is sufficient.
    🔗
    Symbolically,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \neg(\forall x\,P(x))\equiv\exists x\,\neg P(x). \end{equation*}

    Subsection 1.8.37 Mathematical Induction

    Many statements concern every natural number.
    🔗
    Mathematical induction provides a systematic method for proving such statements.
    🔗

    Definition 1.8.21. Mathematical Induction.

    Suppose \(P(n)\) is a statement defined for natural numbers. To prove
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(n)\text{ for all }n\geq n_0, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    it is sufficient to show:
    🔗
    1. \(P(n_0)\) is true;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. for every \(k\geq n_0\text{,}\) \(P(k)\to P(k+1)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The first step is called the base case.
    🔗
    The second is called the inductive step.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.38 The Inductive Hypothesis

    In the inductive step, one assumes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(k) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    for an arbitrary integer \(k\geq n_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This assumption is called the inductive hypothesis.
    🔗
    The goal is then to prove
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(k+1). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    One does not assume that \(P(k+1)\) is true.
    🔗
    That is precisely what must be established.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.39 Why Induction Works

    Induction can be visualized as a chain.
    🔗
    The base case establishes the first link.
    🔗
    The inductive step proves that whenever one link holds, the next one follows:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(n_0)\to P(n_0+1)\to P(n_0+2)\to\cdots. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus every subsequent statement is reached.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.40 Worked Example: Sum of the First \(n\) Integers

    Example 1.8.22. Prove a Summation Formula.

    Prove that for every \(n\in\N\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Base case: @@PROTECTED0@@.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1=\frac{1(1+1)}2=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So the statement holds for \(n=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Inductive hypothesis.
    🔗
    Assume for some \(k\in\N\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1+2+\cdots+k=\frac{k(k+1)}2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Inductive step.
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1+2+\cdots+k+(k+1) &=\frac{k(k+1)}2+(k+1)\\ &=(k+1)\left(\frac{k}{2}+1\right)\\ &=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}2. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}

    This is exactly the desired formula with \(n=k+1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2 \qquad\text{for all }n\in\N. \end{equation*}

    🔗
    🔗

    If a statement is intended for every integer

    \begin{equation*} n\geq5, \end{equation*}

    Subsection 1.8.43 Strong Induction

    Definition 1.8.23. Strong Induction.

    In strong induction, the inductive step assumes that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(n_0),P(n_0+1),\ldots,P(k) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    are all true and uses these assumptions to prove \(P(k+1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Strong induction is logically equivalent in power to ordinary induction, but it is often more convenient when the next case depends on several earlier cases.
    🔗
    🔗

    Suppose a sequence satisfies
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To reason about \(a_{k+1}\text{,}\) one may need information about both
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a_k \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a_{k-1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Strong induction naturally allows access to all previous cases.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.8.51 Common Proof Errors

    Subsubsection 1.8.51.1 Proving by Example

    Checking
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n=1,2,3,4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    does not prove a statement for every integer.
    🔗
    Examples can suggest a theorem but do not establish a universal claim.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.8.51.2 Assuming What Must Be Proved

    A proof cannot assume its desired conclusion unless that assumption is part of a legitimate contradiction argument.
    🔗
    Using the conclusion as justification for itself is called circular reasoning.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.8.51.3 Using the Converse

    From
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P\to Q \end{equation*}
    🔗
    one cannot automatically use
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Q\to P. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The converse must be established independently.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.8.51.4 Dividing by a Possibly Zero Quantity

    A step such as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ax=ay\Longrightarrow x=y \end{equation*}
    🔗
    requires
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Without that condition, the conclusion may fail.
    🔗
    Every cancellation or division step must respect the relevant restrictions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.8.51.5 Using an Unproved Special Case as a General Result

    A statement shown for a particular object cannot automatically be generalized.
    🔗
    The proof must explain why the argument applies to an arbitrary object in the required domain.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.8.51.6 Incomplete Case Analysis

    A proof by cases must cover every possibility.
    🔗
    If a real-number proof considers only
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x>0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x<0, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    it has omitted
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.8.51.7 Misusing the Inductive Hypothesis

    In induction, one may assume \(P(k)\) while proving \(P(k+1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    One may not assume
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(k+1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    itself.
    🔗
    Doing so would make the argument circular.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof methods are used throughout the remainder of mathematics. Direct proof, contraposition, contradiction, and induction appear repeatedly in algebra, number theory, analysis, topology, probability, and discrete mathematics. Set proofs and function proofs provide patterns that will recur throughout higher mathematics, while mathematical induction becomes especially important for sequences, combinatorics, recurrence relations, algorithms, and number theory.
