    Subsection 2.1.1 What Is Algebra?

    Arithmetic studies computations involving particular numbers.
    🔗
    Algebra extends arithmetic by replacing particular quantities with symbols.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3(5)+2=17 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is a numerical calculation.
    🔗
    The expression
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    describes an entire family of calculations depending on the value of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{conceptbox} Algebra studies relationships and structures that remain valid even when the specific numerical values change. \end{conceptbox}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.2 The Structural View of Algebra

    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3(x+4). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This expression has two visible layers:
    🔗
    \begin{tikzpicture}[ level distance=10mm, level 1/.style={sibling distance=33mm}, level 2/.style={sibling distance=17mm}, every node/.style={font=\small} ] \node {\(3(x+4)\)} child {node {\(3\)}} child {node {\(x+4\)} child {node {\(x\)}} child {node {\(4\)}}}; \end{tikzpicture}
    🔗
    The outer operation is multiplication.
    🔗
    Inside the parentheses is addition.
    🔗
    Recognizing this structure helps determine which operation should be performed first.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.3 Algebraic Expressions

    An algebraic expression combines numbers, variables, and mathematical operations.
    🔗
    Examples include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+5,\qquad x^2-4x+7,\qquad \frac{x+1}{x-2},\qquad \sqrt{x+4}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    An expression does not assert that something is true or false.
    🔗
    By contrast,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+5=17 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is an equation because it asserts equality.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.4 Terms and Coefficients

    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4x^2-3x+7. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The terms are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4x^2,\qquad -3x,\qquad 7. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The coefficients are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4,\qquad -3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The constant term is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 7. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A coefficient is a numerical factor multiplying a variable expression.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.5 Visual Structure of an Expression

    The expression
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5x^2-2x+9 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    can be organized visually as
    🔗
    \begin{tikzpicture}[ every node/.style={font=\small}, node distance=8mm ] \node (expr) [draw,rounded corners,inner sep=4pt] {\(5x^2-2x+9\)}; \node (t1) [below left=10mm and 18mm of expr] {\(5x^2\)}; \node (t2) [below=10mm of expr] {\(-2x\)}; \node (t3) [below right=10mm and 18mm of expr] {\(9\)}; \draw[->] (expr) -- (t1); \draw[->] (expr) -- (t2); \draw[->] (expr) -- (t3); \node[below=2mm of t1] {\scriptsize quadratic term}; \node[below=2mm of t2] {\scriptsize linear term}; \node[below=2mm of t3] {\scriptsize constant term}; \end{tikzpicture}
    🔗
    This type of structural recognition becomes important when factoring, differentiating, integrating, and studying polynomial behavior.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.6 Like Terms

    Definition 2.1.3. Like Terms.

    Two terms are like terms if their variable factors and exponents are identical.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x^2,\qquad -7x^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    are like terms.
    🔗
    But
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x^2,\qquad 3x \end{equation*}
    🔗
    are not.
    🔗
    Likewise,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2xy^2,\qquad -5xy^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    are like terms.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.7 Combining Like Terms

    Like terms combine using the distributive property.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+7x=(3+7)x=10x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5x^2-2x^2+4x=3x^2+4x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Unlike terms remain separate.
    🔗
    \begin{warningbox} The expression
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+4x^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    does not simplify to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 7x^3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The terms are not like terms. \end{warningbox}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.8 The Distributive Property

    The distributive property states
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(b+c)=ab+ac. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(b-c)=ab-ac. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    It can be interpreted as spreading one factor across every term inside parentheses.
    🔗
    \begin{tikzpicture}[>=stealth,every node/.style={font=\small}] \node (a) at (0,0) {\(a\)}; \node (b) at (2,0.7) {\(b\)}; \node (c) at (2,-0.7) {\(c\)}; \draw[->] (a) -- (b); \draw[->] (a) -- (c); \node at (4,0.7) {\(ab\)}; \node at (4,-0.7) {\(ac\)}; \draw[->] (b) -- (3.3,0.7); \draw[->] (c) -- (3.3,-0.7); \node at (5.2,0) {\(ab+ac\)}; \end{tikzpicture}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.9 Area Interpretation of Distribution

    The distributive property can also be understood geometrically.
    🔗
    A rectangle with height \(a\) and total width \(b+c\) has area
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(b+c). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Dividing it into two smaller rectangles gives areas
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ab \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ac. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85] \draw (0,0) rectangle (6,2); \draw (3.5,0) -- (3.5,2); \node at (1.75,1) {\(ab\)}; \node at (4.75,1) {\(ac\)}; \node[below] at (1.75,0) {\(b\)}; \node[below] at (4.75,0) {\(c\)}; \node[left] at (0,1) {\(a\)}; \end{tikzpicture}
    🔗
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(b+c)=ab+ac. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.10 Negative Distribution

    A negative sign outside parentheses is equivalent to multiplying every term by \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -(x+5)=-x-5, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -(x-5)=-x+5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.11 Worked Example: Simplification

    Example 2.1.4. Simplify an Algebraic Expression.

    Simplify
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3(2x-5)-4(x+1)+7. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Solution.
    🔗

    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.12 Equivalent Expressions

    Two expressions are equivalent when they have the same value for every input for which both are defined.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2(x+3)=2x+6. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    These are different forms of the same expression.
    🔗
    Algebra frequently consists of moving between equivalent representations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.13 Equations

    An equation asserts that two expressions are equal.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+4=16. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A solution is a value making the equation true.
    🔗
    Here,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    because
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3(4)+4=16. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.14 Balance Interpretation of Equations

    An equation may be visualized as a balance.
    🔗
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9] \draw[thick] (-3,0) -- (3,0); \draw[thick] (0,0) -- (0,-1); \draw[thick] (-0.5,-1) -- (0.5,-1); \draw (-2.4,0) -- (-2.4,-0.6); \draw (2.4,0) -- (2.4,-0.6); \draw[thick] (-3,-0.6) -- (-1.8,-0.6); \draw[thick] (1.8,-0.6) -- (3,-0.6); \node at (-2.4,-0.95) {\(x+3\)}; \node at (2.4,-0.95) {\(8\)}; \end{tikzpicture}
    🔗
    If equal quantities are added, subtracted, multiplied, or divided on both sides, the balance is preserved.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.15 Properties of Equality

    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a=b, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+c=b+c, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a-c=b-c, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ac=bc. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If \(c\neq0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac ac=\frac bc. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The nonzero condition matters when dividing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.16 Linear Equations in One Variable

    Definition 2.1.5. Linear Equation in One Variable.

    A linear equation in one variable can be written
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ax+b=c, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(a,b,c\) are constants and \(a\neq0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solving gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ax=c-b, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\frac{c-b}{a}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Solve
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5x-8=27. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Manual conversion required: This table from the LaTeX source still needs to be converted to native PreTeXt.
    🔗
    Solution.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.18 Variables on Both Sides

    Consider

    Subsection 2.1.19 One, None, or Infinitely Many Solutions

    A linear equation may produce different outcomes.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2x+3=9 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    gives one solution.
    🔗
    The equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2(x+3)=2x+6 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    simplifies to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2x+6=2x+6, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    which is true for every \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2(x+3)=2x+8 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    simplifies to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 6=8, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    which is impossible.
    🔗
    Thus a linear equation may have:
    🔗
    • one solution;
      🔗
      🔗
    • infinitely many solutions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • no solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.20 Literal Equations

    A literal equation contains several variables.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=mx+b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solving for \(x\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y-b=mx, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    so, assuming \(m\neq0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\frac{y-b}{m}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.35 Important Algebraic Identities

    Square of a sum:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2. \end{equation*}

    🔗

    🔗
    Sum of cubes:
    🔗

    🔗
    Difference of cubes:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2). \end{equation*}

    Subsection 2.1.37 Factoring

    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+12=3(x+4). \end{equation*}

    🔗

    🔗
    1. factor the greatest common factor;

      🔗
    2. check for special identities;
      🔗
      🔗
    3. factor trinomials if possible;
      🔗
      🔗

      🔗

    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.51 Deriving the Quadratic Formula

    Begin with
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ax^2+bx+c=0,\qquad a\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Divide by \(a\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2+\frac ba x+\frac ca=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Move the constant:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2+\frac ba x=-\frac ca. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Add
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{b^2}{4a^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Taking square roots gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+\frac{b}{2a}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{importantbox}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \end{importantbox}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.52 The Discriminant

    The quantity
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta=b^2-4ac \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is called the discriminant.
    🔗
    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is uppercase Greek delta, pronounced “DEL-tuh.”
    🔗
    The sign of \(\Delta\) determines the number of real roots.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.55 Rational Expressions

    Definition 2.1.14. Rational Expression.

    A rational expression has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(p\) and \(q\) are polynomials and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} q(x)\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=\frac{x+3}{x-5}, \end{equation*}

    the denominator requires
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\neq5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore the real domain is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \R\setminus\{5\}. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 2.1.57 Simplifying Rational Expressions

    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2-9}{x^2+x-6}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Factor:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{(x+3)(x-2)}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2-9}{x^2+x-6}=\frac{x-3}{x-2}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    but the restrictions from the original denominator remain:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\neq-3,\qquad x\neq2. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.59 Cancellation Means Factoring

    Cancellation is valid only for common factors.
    🔗
    Valid:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x(x+3)}{x}=x+3,\qquad x\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Invalid:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x+3}{x}\neq3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The numerator \(x+3\) is a sum, not a product containing a common factor \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.60 Adding Rational Expressions

    A common denominator is required.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac1x+\frac1{x+1} &=\frac{x+1}{x(x+1)}+\frac{x}{x(x+1)}\\ &=\frac{2x+1}{x(x+1)}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Restrictions:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\neq0,\qquad x\neq-1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.61 Rational Equations

    A rational equation may be solved by multiplying every term by a common denominator.
    🔗
    Restrictions must be identified first.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.63 Radicals

    The expression
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[n]{a} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is called the \(n\)-th root of \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The number \(n\) is the index.
    🔗
    The quantity \(a\) is the radicand.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[3]{8}=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.64 Even and Odd Roots

    For real numbers, an even root requires a nonnegative radicand.
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is real only when
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\geq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    An odd root permits negative values:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[3]{-8}=-2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    The graph of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=\sqrt{x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    begins at the origin and exists only for
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\geq0. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 2.1.66 Principal Square Root

    The notation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt a \end{equation*}
    🔗
    means the nonnegative principal square root.
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{25}=5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    But the equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=25 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    has two solutions:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\pm5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.67 Simplifying Radicals

    Perfect powers may be extracted.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36\cdot2}=6\sqrt2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[3]{54}=\sqrt[3]{27\cdot2}=3\sqrt[3]{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.68 Rational Exponents

    For suitable real values,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    More generally,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 16^{3/4}=8. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.69 Rationalizing Denominators

    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac1{\sqrt2} =\frac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2} =\frac{\sqrt2}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If the denominator contains two terms, a conjugate may be useful.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.70 Conjugates

    The conjugate of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+b \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a-b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Their product is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (\sqrt3+1)(\sqrt3-1)=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    An extraneous solution is produced by algebraic manipulation but does not satisfy the original equation.

      🔗
      🔗
    • clearing denominators;
      🔗

    Subsection 2.1.76 Common Algebra Errors

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.1 Combining Unlike Terms

    Incorrect:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+4x^2=7x^3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Correct:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x+4x^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    cannot be combined further.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.2 Incorrect Distribution

    Incorrect:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3(x+4)=3x+4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Correct:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3(x+4)=3x+12. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.3 Incorrect Squaring

    Incorrect:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)^2=a^2+b^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Correct:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.4 Canceling Terms Instead of Factors

    Incorrect:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x+4}{x}=4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Cancellation applies to factors, not terms inside a sum.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.5 Dividing by Zero

    Any division by an expression \(u\) requires
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.6 Forgetting to Reverse an Inequality

    Dividing by a negative reverses the inequality.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.7 Forgetting Plus-or-Minus

    From
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=16, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    one must conclude
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\pm4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.8 Confusing \(\sqrt{x^2}\) with \(x\)

    For real \(x\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{x^2}=|x|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{(-4)^2}=4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.76.9 Ignoring Domain Restrictions

    A canceled factor may remove visible evidence of a domain restriction, but the original restriction remains.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.78 Section Connections

    Remark 2.1.50. Chapter Connection.

    Elementary algebra supplies the symbolic language used throughout nearly all later mathematics. Linear equations lead naturally into systems and linear algebra. Polynomial structure leads toward abstract algebra, ring theory, and field theory. Quadratic graphs connect algebra with analytic geometry and calculus. Rational expressions introduce domain restrictions, holes, and asymptotic behavior that later become important in limits and analysis. Radicals and rational exponents connect algebra with functions, complex numbers, and higher-degree equations.
