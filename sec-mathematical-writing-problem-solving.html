    Section 1.10 Mathematical Writing and Problem Solving

    Remark 1.10.1. Prerequisites.

    Familiarity with mathematical notation, sets, functions, logic, and proof methods from the preceding sections of Chapter 0.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.10.2. Learning Objectives.

    By the end of this section, the reader should be able to:
    🔗
    • read mathematical writing actively rather than mechanically;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use definitions as working mathematical tools;
      🔗
      🔗
    • write mathematical expressions as parts of complete sentences;
      🔗
      🔗
    • introduce variables and notation clearly;
      🔗
      🔗
    • organize displayed equations and derivations effectively;
      🔗
      🔗
    • translate between mathematical notation and spoken language;
      🔗
      🔗
    • identify the givens, unknowns, conditions, and goals of a problem;
      🔗
      🔗
    • apply a systematic problem-solving process;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use diagrams, tables, patterns, and special cases strategically;
      🔗
      🔗
    • work both forward from known information and backward from a goal;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use estimation, units, domains, and special cases to check results;
      🔗
      🔗
    • distinguish exploratory work from a polished mathematical solution;
      🔗
      🔗
    • write clear proofs and worked solutions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • recognize common weaknesses in mathematical communication.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.1 Mathematics as Communication

    Mathematics is not merely a collection of calculations.
    🔗
    It is a language for expressing definitions, relationships, patterns, arguments, structures, and conclusions precisely.
    🔗
    A correct calculation can still be difficult to understand if its notation is unclear or its reasoning is poorly organized.
    🔗
    Likewise, a well-written mathematical argument allows another reader to reconstruct not only what was done, but why each step is valid.
    🔗
    \begin{conceptbox} Good mathematical writing aims for three qualities:
    🔗
    • correctness --- every statement is mathematically valid;
      🔗
      🔗
    • clarity --- the reader can understand what each symbol and step means;
      🔗
      🔗
    • precision --- assumptions, domains, definitions, and conclusions are stated accurately.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    \end{conceptbox}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.2 Reading Mathematics Actively

    Mathematical reading is usually slower than ordinary reading.
    🔗
    A single displayed equation may contain several relationships that must be interpreted before continuing.
    🔗
    When reading mathematics, ask:
    🔗
    • What objects are being discussed?
      🔗
      🔗
    • What do the symbols represent?
      🔗
      🔗
    • What is assumed?
      🔗
      🔗
    • What is being claimed?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Which definitions are being used?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Why does one step follow from the previous step?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Is the statement an example, definition, theorem, or conclusion?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Reading actively often requires stopping and reconstructing omitted intermediate reasoning.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.3 Reading Symbols as Meaning

    Symbols should not be read merely as shapes.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\in A \end{equation*}
    🔗
    should be understood as
    🔗
    “@@PROTECTED0@@ is an element of @@PROTECTED1@@.”
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\subseteq B \end{equation*}
    🔗
    means
    🔗
    “every element of @@PROTECTED0@@ is also an element of @@PROTECTED1@@.”
    🔗
    The symbolic form is compact, but the underlying meaning should remain accessible.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.4 Reading Mathematical Structure

    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A reader should notice more than the numerator and denominator.
    🔗
    The expression has a restriction:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\neq1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The numerator factors:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus, for \(x\neq1\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=x+1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The restriction remains important even after simplification.
    🔗
    Active reading identifies both algebraic structure and domain information.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.5 Definitions Are Working Tools

    Definitions should not be treated as vocabulary to memorize and then ignore.
    🔗
    They tell us exactly what must be shown.
    🔗
    Suppose one wants to prove that a function is injective.
    🔗
    The definition says that one must establish
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x_1)=f(x_2)\Longrightarrow x_1=x_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    That definition immediately suggests the structure of the proof.
    🔗
    Similarly, to prove
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\subseteq B, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    the definition tells us to begin with an arbitrary
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\in A \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and establish
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\in B. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.6 Unpacking Definitions

    A useful mathematical habit is to replace terminology with its precise definition.
    🔗
    For example:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{c|l} \text{Term} & \text{Useful Mathematical Form}\\ \hline n\text{ is even} & n=2k\text{ for some }k\in\Z\\ n\text{ is odd} & n=2k+1\text{ for some }k\in\Z\\ A\subseteq B & x\in A\Rightarrow x\in B\\ f\text{ injective} & f(x_1)=f(x_2)\Rightarrow x_1=x_2\\ f\text{ surjective} & \forall y\in B,\;\exists x\in A,\;f(x)=y \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Definitions convert abstract words into usable mathematical conditions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.7 Mathematical Sentences

    Mathematical expressions should usually function as parts of grammatical sentences.
    🔗
    For example:
    🔗
    Since \(x>0\text{,}\) we have \(x^2>0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This is preferable to an unexplained sequence such as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x>0,\qquad x^2>0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The second version contains mathematical information but does not communicate the logical relationship between the statements.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.8 Symbols Do Not Replace Explanation

    Symbolic notation is powerful because it compresses information.
    🔗
    However, excessive symbolic compression can obscure reasoning.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\in A\Rightarrow x\in B\Rightarrow A\subseteq B \end{equation*}
    🔗
    may be understandable in context, but a proof is clearer when the role of the arbitrary element is stated:
    🔗
    Let \(x\in A\) be arbitrary. If \(x\in A\text{,}\) then \(x\in B\text{.}\) Therefore every element of \(A\) belongs to \(B\text{,}\) so \(A\subseteq B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.9 Introducing Variables

    A variable should be introduced before it is used.
    🔗
    Instead of writing
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n=2k \end{equation*}
    🔗
    without context, write:
    🔗
    Let \(n\) be an even integer. Then \(n=2k\) for some \(k\in\Z\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The reader now knows:
    🔗
    • what \(n\) represents;
      🔗
      🔗
    • why \(k\) exists;
      🔗
      🔗
    • what set \(k\) belongs to.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.10 Declare the Domain

    A variable’s domain can fundamentally change the meaning of a statement.
    🔗
    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Over \(\R\text{,}\) this equation has solutions
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\pm\sqrt2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Over \(\Q\text{,}\) it has no solutions.
    🔗
    Therefore statements such as
    🔗
    “Let @@PROTECTED0@@ be a real number”
    🔗
    or
    🔗
    “Let @@PROTECTED0@@”
    🔗
    often carry essential mathematical information.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.11 Introduce Specialized Notation

    When notation is created for a local argument, explain it.
    🔗
    For example:
    🔗
    Let \(E\) denote the set of even integers.
    🔗
    Then one may write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} E=\{2k:k\in\Z\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The notation should help the reader rather than force the reader to guess its meaning.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.12 Use Notation Consistently

    Once a symbol has been assigned a meaning, that meaning should remain stable throughout the argument unless explicitly redefined.
    🔗
    If \(n\) denotes an integer at the beginning of a proof, it should not later become a real-valued function without explanation.
    🔗
    Consistency reduces unnecessary cognitive effort.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.13 Displayed Mathematics

    Important equations are often placed on their own line:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ax+b=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Displayed mathematics is useful when:
    🔗
    • an equation is central to the discussion;
      🔗
      🔗
    • several transformations must be shown;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the expression is too long for comfortable inline reading;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the reader needs to refer visually to the equation.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Short expressions can remain inline when displaying them would interrupt the flow unnecessarily.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.14 Aligned Derivations

    When several equations form one calculation, alignment can reveal the structure.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} (x+3)^2 &=(x+3)(x+3)\\ &=x^2+3x+3x+9\\ &=x^2+6x+9. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The equality signs align vertically, making the chain of equivalent expressions easy to follow.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.15 Justifying Important Steps

    Not every elementary algebraic step needs a paragraph of explanation.
    🔗
    However, important transitions should be justified.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^2=b^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    does not imply only
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a=b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The correct conclusion over the real numbers is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a=b\qquad\text{or}\qquad a=-b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A written solution should make such logical branches visible.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.16 Equality Signs Must Mean Equality

    The equality symbol should connect expressions that are actually equal.
    🔗
    A common poor habit is to write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2-4=0=x^2=4=x=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This is not a valid chain of equalities.
    🔗
    A clearer derivation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} x^2-4&=0\\ x^2&=4\\ x&=\pm2. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Each line now follows meaningfully from the previous one.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.17 The Problem-Solving Process

    Problem solving is rarely a perfectly linear process.
    🔗
    Nevertheless, a useful general framework is:
    🔗
    1. understand the problem;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. organize the information;
      🔗
      🔗
    3. choose a strategy;
      🔗
      🔗
    4. carry out the strategy;
      🔗
      🔗
    5. check the result;
      🔗
      🔗
    6. communicate the solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    These stages may repeat.
    🔗
    A failed strategy often provides information that suggests a better one.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.18 Step 1: Understand the Problem

    Before calculating, determine what the problem actually asks.
    🔗
    Identify:
    🔗
    • the known quantities;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the unknown quantities;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the assumptions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the domain;
      🔗
      🔗
    • any restrictions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the desired conclusion.

    A surprising number of errors begin with solving a different problem from the one that was asked.

    Subsection 1.10.19 Identify the Goal

    Subsection 1.10.26 Work Forward

    Working forward means beginning with known information and determining what follows.
    🔗
    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x>3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then one may deduce
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+2>5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If \(x\) is known to be positive, one may also deduce
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2>0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Forward reasoning asks:
    🔗
    “What can I conclude from what I already know?”
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.27 Work Backward

    Working backward begins with the desired conclusion and asks what would be sufficient to establish it.
    🔗
    Suppose the goal is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (x-2)^2=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    One may ask:
    🔗
    What condition would make this true?
    🔗
    Clearly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x-2=0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    would be sufficient, which suggests
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Backward reasoning is especially useful during problem discovery.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.28 Meet in the Middle

    Many difficult problems are solved by combining forward and backward reasoning.
    🔗
    From the hypotheses, derive useful consequences.
    🔗
    From the goal, determine what intermediate result would be sufficient.
    🔗
    Then search for a connection between the two.
    🔗
    This strategy is common in proofs, geometry, algebra, and analysis.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.29 Break a Problem into Smaller Parts

    A complicated problem often becomes manageable when decomposed.
    🔗
    For example, to analyze
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x^2-x-6}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    one might separately:
    🔗
    1. factor the numerator;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. factor the denominator;
      🔗
      🔗
    3. determine domain restrictions;
      🔗
      🔗
    4. simplify common factors;
      🔗
      🔗
    5. interpret the remaining expression.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Each smaller task is easier than handling the entire expression at once.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.30 Draw a Diagram

    A diagram can reveal relationships that are difficult to see symbolically.
    🔗
    Diagrams are particularly useful in:
    🔗
    • geometry;
      🔗
      🔗
    • trigonometry;
      🔗
      🔗
    • vector analysis;
      🔗
      🔗
    • graph theory;
      🔗
      🔗
    • probability;
      🔗
      🔗
    • optimization;
      🔗
      🔗
    • multivariable calculus.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    A diagram should support reasoning rather than replace it.
    🔗
    A picture may suggest that something is true without proving it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.31 Make a Table

    Tables organize repeated calculations and can reveal patterns.
    🔗
    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a_n=n^2-n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Evaluating several values gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{c|ccccc} n&1&2&3&4&5\\ \hline a_n&0&2&6&12&20 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The values suggest
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a_n=n(n-1), \end{equation*}
    🔗
    which is always even because one of two consecutive integers is even.
    🔗
    The table helps discover the pattern; a proof establishes it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.32 Search for Patterns

    Patterns often suggest conjectures.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1=1^2, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1+3=4=2^2, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1+3+5=9=3^2, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1+3+5+7=16=4^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This suggests
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The observed pattern motivates the statement.
    🔗
    Mathematical induction can then prove it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.33 Conjectures

    A conjecture is a mathematical statement believed to be true based on evidence or reasoning but not yet established by proof.
    🔗
    Experimentation is valuable for producing conjectures.
    🔗
    However,
    🔗
    \begin{importantbox} A pattern suggests a theorem; it does not prove a theorem. \end{importantbox}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.34 Test Special Cases

    Special cases can clarify a general problem.
    🔗
    If a statement involves a parameter \(n\text{,}\) test values such as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n=1,\qquad n=2,\qquad n=3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If it involves a real variable, useful cases may include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=0,\qquad x=1,\qquad x=-1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Special cases can:
    🔗
    • reveal errors;
      🔗
      🔗
    • suggest patterns;
      🔗
      🔗
    • identify missing assumptions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • produce counterexamples.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.35 Look for Counterexamples

    Before attempting to prove a statement, ask whether it is actually true.
    🔗
    Consider the claim
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{x^2}=x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Testing
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=-3 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{(-3)^2}=3\neq-3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus the claim is false.
    🔗
    The correct identity is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{x^2}=|x|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A small test can prevent a long attempt to prove a false statement.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.36 Estimate Before Calculating

    Estimation gives a rough expectation for the answer.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 19.8(5.1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    should be close to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 20(5)=100. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If a calculator returns
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 10.098, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    the result should immediately appear suspicious.
    🔗
    Estimation provides a simple error-detection mechanism.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.37 Exact and Approximate Answers

    Whenever practical, preserve exact values until approximation is needed.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is exact, whereas
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1.414 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is approximate.
    🔗
    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \approx \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read
    🔗
    “is approximately equal to.”
    🔗
    Thus one may write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2\approx1.414. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Using \(=\) instead of \(\approx\) would incorrectly claim exact equality.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.38 Check Units

    Units provide mathematical information.
    🔗
    Suppose distance is measured in meters and time in seconds.
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    has units
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\mathrm m}{\mathrm s}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A proposed speed with units
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm m^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    would therefore signal an error.
    🔗
    This type of reasoning is called dimensional analysis in many applied contexts.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.39 Unit Consistency

    Quantities being added or subtracted should have compatible units.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3\text{ meters}+5\text{ meters}=8\text{ meters} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is meaningful.
    🔗
    But
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3\text{ meters}+5\text{ seconds} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    does not represent an ordinary physical sum.
    🔗
    Unit consistency becomes especially important in applied mathematics, physics, engineering, and mathematical modeling.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.40 Check the Domain

    Algebraic manipulation may produce values that are not allowed in the original problem.
    🔗
    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x+1}{x-2}=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The denominator requires
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\neq2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The equation is zero when the numerator is zero:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=-1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The domain restriction must remain part of the reasoning.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.41 Extraneous Solutions

    Some operations can introduce candidate solutions that do not satisfy the original equation.
    🔗
    For example, squaring both sides is not always reversible.
    🔗
    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=-2, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    But starting from
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    does not allow one to conclude only \(x=-2\text{;}\) both signs are possible.
    🔗
    In more complicated equations, squaring can produce extraneous solutions.
    🔗
    Therefore candidate solutions should often be checked in the original equation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.42 Reversible and Irreversible Steps

    An equivalence transformation preserves exactly the same solution set.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+3=7\Longleftrightarrow x=4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    However, an implication may preserve only one direction.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=2\Longrightarrow x^2=4, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    but
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=4\not\Longrightarrow x=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The distinction between implication and equivalence is important when solving equations and writing proofs.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.43 Reading Advanced Notation

    As mathematics becomes more advanced, notation becomes increasingly compact.
    🔗
    The goal should remain to understand the mathematical sentence represented by the symbols.
    🔗
    For example, later in calculus one encounters
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The notation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read “limit.”
    🔗
    In this context, the arrow
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\to a \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read
    🔗
    “@@PROTECTED0@@ approaches @@PROTECTED1@@.”
    🔗
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to a}f(x) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read
    🔗
    “the limit of @@PROTECTED0@@ as @@PROTECTED1@@ approaches @@PROTECTED2@@.”
    🔗
    The complete statement
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read
    🔗
    “the limit of @@PROTECTED0@@ as @@PROTECTED1@@ approaches @@PROTECTED2@@ equals @@PROTECTED3@@.”
    🔗
    The rigorous meaning of limits is developed later in Analysis.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.44 Context Determines How Symbols Are Read

    The same symbol can have different readings in different mathematical contexts.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P\to Q \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read
    🔗
    “@@PROTECTED0@@ implies @@PROTECTED1@@.”
    🔗
    For a function,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f:A\to B \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read
    🔗
    “@@PROTECTED0@@ maps @@PROTECTED1@@ to @@PROTECTED2@@.”
    🔗
    In calculus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\to a \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is read
    🔗
    “@@PROTECTED0@@ approaches @@PROTECTED1@@.”
    🔗
    The shape of a symbol alone does not determine its interpretation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.45 Writing Proofs Clearly

    A proof should communicate its logical structure.
    🔗
    For example:
    🔗
    Let \(n\) be an even integer. By definition, \(n=2k\) for some \(k\in\Z\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n^2=(2k)^2=4k^2=2(2k^2). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(2k^2\in\Z\text{,}\) \(n^2\) is even.
    🔗
    The proof contains:
    🔗
    • an arbitrary object;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the relevant definition;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the calculation;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the final connection back to the definition.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.46 Do Not Hide the Conclusion

    A proof should clearly indicate when the desired result has been established.
    🔗
    Phrases such as
    🔗
    “Therefore @@PROTECTED0@@ is even”
    🔗
    or
    🔗
    “Hence @@PROTECTED0@@”
    🔗
    help the reader connect the final step with the original claim.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.47 Writing Worked Solutions

    A good worked solution should answer three questions:
    🔗
    1. What are we trying to find?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Why is each important step valid?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. What is the final answer?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The amount of explanation depends on the problem and intended audience.
    🔗
    Elementary algebra may require more intermediate steps than an advanced analysis calculation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.10.53.1 Undefined Variables

    Poor:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2=r^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Better:
    🔗
    Let \(x\) and \(y\) denote the legs of a right triangle and let \(r\) denote the length of its hypotenuse. Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2=r^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.10.53.2 Ambiguous Pronouns

    Statements such as
    🔗
    “This shows it is true”
    🔗
    may be unclear when several objects have just been discussed.
    🔗
    Name the object or conclusion explicitly.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.10.53.3 Unexplained Equations

    A long chain of equations without explanation may leave the reader unable to identify the purpose of the calculation.
    🔗
    Use brief prose to identify important steps.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.10.53.4 Too Much Explanation

    Clarity does not require explaining every trivial arithmetic operation.
    🔗
    Writing
    🔗
    “Now add @@PROTECTED0@@ to @@PROTECTED1@@, which produces @@PROTECTED2@@”
    🔗
    may be unnecessary in an advanced text.
    🔗
    The appropriate level of explanation depends on the mathematical difficulty and intended audience.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.10.53.5 Incorrect Use of Equality

    Do not use \(=\) to mean:
    🔗
    • “therefore”;
      🔗
      🔗
    • “approximately”;
      🔗
      🔗
    • “the next step is”;
      🔗
      🔗
    • “implies.”
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Each of these ideas has its own mathematical or linguistic expression.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.10.53.6 Ignoring Restrictions

    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=\frac1{x-3}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\neq3 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is part of the mathematical information.
    🔗
    A solution that ignores the restriction is incomplete.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.10.53.7 Premature Decimal Approximation

    Replacing exact values by rounded decimals too early can introduce accumulated error.
    🔗
