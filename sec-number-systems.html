    Section 1.3 Number Systems

    Prerequisites. Familiarity with mathematical notation from Section 1.1 and arithmetic from Section 1.2.
    🔗

    Objectives: Learning Objectives

    By the end of this section, the reader should be able to:
    🔗
    • distinguish natural, whole, integer, rational, irrational, real, and complex numbers;
      🔗
      🔗
    • classify numbers using the smallest standard number system that contains them;
      🔗
      🔗
    • interpret terminating and repeating decimals;
      🔗
      🔗
    • convert repeating decimals to fractions;
      🔗
      🔗
    • interpret absolute value as distance;
      🔗
      🔗
    • use interval notation;
      🔗
      🔗
    • work with complex numbers in standard form;
      🔗
      🔗
    • compute complex sums, products, quotients, conjugates, and moduli;
      🔗
      🔗
    • understand the containment relationships among the standard number systems;
      🔗
      🔗
    • recognize closure properties and the reason number systems are extended.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.1 Why Number Systems Expand

    The history and structure of mathematics repeatedly show the same pattern: an existing number system is sufficient for some operations but not for others.
    🔗
    The natural numbers are sufficient for counting:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1,2,3,\ldots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    but subtraction may leave the natural numbers:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3-5=-2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The integers include negative values, but division may leave the integers:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1\div2=\frac12. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The rational numbers include fractions, but equations such as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    require numbers that cannot be represented as ratios of integers.
    🔗
    The real numbers include those irrational values, but the equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=-1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    has no real solution.
    🔗
    This motivates a sequence of increasingly large number systems.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.2 The Natural Numbers

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \N \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is the blackboard-bold capital N and denotes the natural numbers.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.1. Natural Numbers.

    In this text, the natural numbers are the positive counting numbers:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \N=\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The ellipsis indicates that the counting pattern continues indefinitely.
    🔗

    Warning 1.3.2. Convention for the Natural Numbers.

    Some authors include \(0\) in the natural numbers.
    🔗
    In this text,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    so \(0\notin\N\) unless another convention is explicitly stated.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.3 The Whole Numbers

    Definition 1.3.3. Whole Numbers.

    The whole numbers are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This terminology is common in elementary mathematics.
    🔗
    In more advanced mathematics, the natural numbers and integers are usually more important as formal number systems, so a special standard symbol for the whole numbers is used less consistently.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.4 The Integers

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Z \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is the blackboard-bold capital Z and denotes the integers.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.4. Integers.

    The integers are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Z=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The letter \(\Z\) is traditionally associated with the German word Zahlen, meaning numbers.
    🔗
    The integers extend the natural numbers by including zero and the negative whole numbers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.5 Opposites

    Definition 1.3.5. Opposite.

    Every integer \(a\) has an opposite, or additive inverse, written
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -a. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The two numbers satisfy
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+(-a)=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 8+(-8)=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Opposites lie the same distance from zero on the real number line but on opposite sides.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.6 The Rational Numbers

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Q \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is the blackboard-bold capital Q and denotes the rational numbers.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.6. Rational Number.

    A number is rational if it can be written in the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a}{b}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a,b\in\Z,\qquad b\neq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Q= \left\{ \frac{a}{b}:a,b\in\Z,\ b\neq0 \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The letter \(Q\) may be remembered through the word quotient.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.7 Examples of Rational Numbers

    Every integer is rational because
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} n=\frac{n}{1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5=\frac51, \qquad -12=\frac{-12}{1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Other examples include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac23, \qquad -\frac74, \qquad \frac{19}{8}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Zero is rational because
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0=\frac01. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.8 Decimal Representations of Rational Numbers

    Every rational number has a decimal expansion that either terminates or eventually repeats.
    🔗
    Examples of terminating decimals include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac12=0.5, \qquad \frac18=0.125. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Examples of repeating decimals include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac13=0.333\ldots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{11}=0.181818\ldots. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.9 Repeating Decimal Notation

    A bar may be placed over repeating digits.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0.\overline3 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    means
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0.3333\ldots. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The horizontal bar is called an overline.
    🔗
    In this context, the expression is read “zero point three repeating.”
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0.\overline{27}=0.27272727\ldots. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.10 Converting a Repeating Decimal to a Fraction

    Repeating decimals can be converted to fractions algebraically.
    🔗

    Example 1.3.7. Convert a Repeating Decimal to a Fraction.

    Convert
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0.\overline3 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    to a fraction.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} 0.\overline3=\frac13 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.11 Longer Repeating Blocks

    If several digits repeat, multiply by an appropriate power of \(10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For example, let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=0.\overline{27}=0.272727\ldots. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Because two digits repeat, multiply by \(100\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 100x=27.272727\ldots. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Subtracting the original equation gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 99x=27. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\frac{27}{99}=\frac3{11}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.12 Irrational Numbers

    Definition 1.3.9. Irrational Number.

    An irrational number is a real number that cannot be written as a ratio of two integers.
    🔗
    🔗
    Examples include
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2, \qquad \sqrt3, \qquad \pi, \qquad e. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Their decimal expansions do not terminate and do not eventually repeat.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.13 Rational versus Irrational

    Every real number is either rational or irrational.
    🔗
    No real number can be both.
    🔗
    Thus, within the real numbers,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{rational} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{irrational} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    form mutually exclusive categories.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.14 Why the Square Root of Two Is Irrational

    The number \(\sqrt2\) is one of the classic examples of an irrational number.
    🔗
    A complete proof uses contradiction.
    🔗
    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2=\frac ab \end{equation*}
    🔗
    for integers \(a,b\) in lowest terms.
    🔗
    Squaring gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2=\frac{a^2}{b^2}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^2=2b^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(a^2\) is even, which forces \(a\) to be even.
    🔗
    Write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a=2k. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4k^2=2b^2, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} b^2=2k^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore \(b\) is also even.
    🔗
    But then \(a\) and \(b\) share a factor of \(2\text{,}\) contradicting the assumption that \(a/b\) was in lowest terms.
    🔗
    Hence,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt2\notin\Q. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The proof method used here is developed formally in Section 1.8.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.15 The Real Numbers

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \R \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is the blackboard-bold capital R and denotes the real numbers.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.10. Real Numbers.

    The real numbers consist of all rational and irrational numbers.
    🔗
    🔗
    The real numbers correspond to the points on the real number line.
    🔗
    Thus numbers such as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -4, \qquad 0, \qquad \frac35, \qquad \sqrt2, \qquad \pi \end{equation*}
    🔗
    are all real.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.16 The Real Number Line

    The real number line gives a geometric interpretation of \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Numbers increase as one moves to the right and decrease as one moves to the left.
    🔗
    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a<b, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    then \(a\) lies to the left of \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a>b, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    then \(a\) lies to the right of \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.17 Absolute Value as Distance

    Definition 1.3.11. Absolute Value.

    The absolute value of a real number \(x\text{,}\) written \(|x|\text{,}\) is its distance from zero on the real number line.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |5|=5, \qquad |-5|=5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Absolute value is always nonnegative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |x|\geq0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.18 Distance Between Real Numbers

    The distance between two real numbers \(a\) and \(b\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |a-b|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Equivalently,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |a-b|=|b-a|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus the order of the two points does not affect their distance.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.19 The Triangle Inequality

    Absolute value satisfies one of the most important inequalities in mathematics.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.12. Triangle Inequality.

    For all real numbers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |a+b|\leq|a|+|b|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Geometrically, the direct distance between two points cannot exceed the distance obtained by traveling through an intermediate point.
    🔗
    This inequality later becomes fundamental in analysis, vector spaces, metric spaces, and functional analysis.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.20 Interval Notation

    Interval notation provides a compact way to describe sets of real numbers.
    🔗
    The open interval
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a,b) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    represents
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \{x\in\R:a<x<b\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Parentheses indicate that the endpoints are not included.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.21 Closed Intervals

    The closed interval
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} [a,b] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    represents
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \{x\in\R:a\leq x\leq b\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Square brackets indicate that the endpoints are included.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.22 Half-Open Intervals

    An interval may include one endpoint but exclude the other.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} [a,b) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    means
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\leq x<b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a,b] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    means
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a<x\leq b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.23 Infinity

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \infty \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is called the infinity symbol.
    🔗
    In ordinary real-number arithmetic, infinity is not itself a real number.
    🔗
    Rather, it is used to describe quantities or sets that are unbounded.
    🔗
    Likewise, \(-\infty\) describes unbounded behavior in the negative direction.
    🔗

    Warning 1.3.13. Infinity Is Not an Ordinary Real Number.

    Expressions involving infinity must be interpreted according to context.
    🔗
    One should not automatically manipulate \(\infty\) as though it were an ordinary element of \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.24 Unbounded Intervals

    The interval
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} [2,\infty) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    represents
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \{x\in\R:x\geq2\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (-\infty,5) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    represents
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \{x\in\R:x<5\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Parentheses are always used next to \(\infty\) and \(-\infty\) because infinity is not an endpoint contained in \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.25 The Need for Complex Numbers

    Consider the equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=-1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    No real number satisfies this equation because the square of every nonzero real number is positive, while
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0^2=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To solve such equations, the real number system is extended to the complex numbers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.26 The Imaginary Unit

    Definition 1.3.14. Imaginary Unit.

    The imaginary unit, written \(i\text{,}\) is defined by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i^2=-1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The symbol \(i\) therefore allows expressions involving square roots of negative real numbers to be represented within a larger number system.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.27 The Complex Numbers

    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \C \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is the blackboard-bold capital C and denotes the complex numbers.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.15. Complex Number.

    A complex number is a number of the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+bi, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(a,b\in\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \C=\{a+bi:a,b\in\R\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.28 Real and Imaginary Parts

    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=a+bi, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    then \(a\) is called the real part of \(z\) and \(b\) is called the imaginary part.
    🔗
    These are written
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Re}(z)=a, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=b. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Warning 1.3.16. The Imaginary Part.

    By standard convention, the imaginary part of \(a+bi\) is \(b\text{,}\) not \(bi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.29 Real Numbers as Complex Numbers

    Every real number can be viewed as a complex number with imaginary part zero:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a=a+0i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \R\subset\C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A number having real part zero is called purely imaginary and has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} bi. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.30 Equality of Complex Numbers

    Two complex numbers are equal exactly when their corresponding real and imaginary parts are equal.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+bi=c+di \end{equation*}
    🔗
    if and only if
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a=c \qquad\text{and}\qquad b=d. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.31 Complex Addition

    Complex numbers are added by combining corresponding real and imaginary parts:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (3+4i)+(2-7i)=5-3i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.32 Complex Subtraction

    Similarly,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+bi)-(c+di) = (a-c)+(b-d)i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (5+2i)-(3-4i)=2+6i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.33 Powers of the Imaginary Unit

    The first four positive powers of \(i\) are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i^1=i, \qquad i^2=-1, \qquad i^3=-i, \qquad i^4=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    These values repeat in a cycle of length four.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i^5=i, \qquad i^6=-1, \qquad i^7=-i, \qquad i^8=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore large powers of \(i\) can be simplified by determining the exponent modulo \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.34 Complex Multiplication

    Complex multiplication follows the distributive property together with
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i^2=-1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+bi)(c+di) = ac+adi+bci+bdi^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(i^2=-1\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.35 Worked Example: Complex Multiplication

    Example 1.3.17. Multiply Complex Numbers.

    Compute
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (2+3i)(4-i). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} 11+10i \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.36 Complex Conjugates

    Definition 1.3.19. Complex Conjugate.

    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=a+bi, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    then the complex conjugate of \(z\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \overline z=a-bi. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, if
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=3+5i, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \overline z=3-5i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.37 Product with a Conjugate

    Multiplying a complex number by its conjugate produces a nonnegative real number:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This follows because the imaginary terms cancel:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^2-abi+abi-b^2i^2 = a^2+b^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This property is especially useful when dividing complex numbers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.38 Complex Division

    To divide by a complex number, multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator.
    🔗
    For
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a+bi}{c+di}, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    with \(c+di\neq0\text{,}\) multiply by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{c-di}{c-di}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The denominator becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} c^2+d^2, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    which is real.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.39 Worked Example: Complex Division

    Example 1.3.20. Divide Complex Numbers.

    Simplify
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2+i}{3-i}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} \frac12+\frac12i \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.40 The Complex Plane

    A complex number
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=a+bi \end{equation*}
    🔗
    can be represented geometrically by the point
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a,b). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The horizontal axis represents the real part and the vertical axis represents the imaginary part.
    🔗
    This coordinate system is called the complex plane or Argand plane.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.41 Complex Modulus

    Definition 1.3.22. Complex Modulus.

    For
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=a+bi, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    the modulus of \(z\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Geometrically, \(|z|\) is the distance from the origin to the point \((a,b)\) in the complex plane.
    🔗
    The same vertical-bar notation used for real absolute value is used for complex modulus.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.42 Worked Example: Conjugate and Modulus

    Example 1.3.23. Find a Conjugate and Modulus.

    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=-3+4i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} \overline z=-3-4i, \qquad |z|=5 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.43 Containment of the Standard Number Systems

    The standard number systems satisfy
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \N\subset\Z\subset\Q\subset\R\subset\C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The symbol
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \subset \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is called a subset symbol and is read “is a subset of.”
    🔗
    Thus every natural number is an integer, every integer is rational, every rational number is real, and every real number is complex.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.44 Classifying Numbers

    A number may belong to several number systems simultaneously.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5\in\N. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Because of the containment chain,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5\in\Z, \qquad 5\in\Q, \qquad 5\in\R, \qquad 5\in\C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    When asked to classify a number, it is often useful to identify the smallest standard number system containing it.
    🔗
    For \(5\text{,}\) that system is \(\N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.45 Worked Example: Classification

    Example 1.3.25. Classify a Rational Number.

    Determine which standard number systems contain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -\frac74. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} -\frac74\in\Q\subset\R\subset\C \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.46 Closure

    Definition 1.3.27. Closure.

    A set is closed under an operation if performing that operation on elements of the set always produces another element of the same set.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, \(\Z\) is closed under addition because
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a,b\in\Z \Longrightarrow a+b\in\Z. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The integers are also closed under subtraction and multiplication.
    🔗
    However, they are not closed under division because
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1,2\in\Z \end{equation*}
    🔗
    but
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac12\notin\Z. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.47 Closure of Familiar Number Systems

    Ignoring operations that are undefined, such as division by zero, a useful summary is:
    🔗
    For division, “yes” means division by a nonzero element remains in the system.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.48 Number Systems and Solving Equations

    The solution to an equation may depend on the number system being used.
    🔗
    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+5=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This has no solution in \(\N\text{,}\) but it has the integer solution
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=-3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2x=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This has no integer solution, but it has the rational solution
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\frac12. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This has no rational solution, but it has real solutions
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\pm\sqrt2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Finally,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=-1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    has no real solution, but it has complex solutions
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\pm i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This progression illustrates why larger number systems are needed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.49 Common Misconceptions

    Every Decimal Is Irrational.

    False.
    🔗
    Terminating and repeating decimals are rational.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0.125=\frac18. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Every Square Root Is Irrational.

    False.
    🔗
    For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt9=3\in\N. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Only some square roots are irrational.
    🔗
    🔗

    Infinity Is a Real Number.

    The symbol \(\infty\) is not an ordinary element of \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    It represents unbounded behavior in contexts such as intervals and limits.
    🔗
    🔗

    The Imaginary Part of a Complex Number.

    By standard convention,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Im}(a+bi)=b, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    not \(bi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Complex Numbers Are Not Real in the Everyday Sense.

    The terms real and complex are technical mathematical names.
    🔗
    Complex numbers are perfectly legitimate mathematical objects.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
